One floods the system with money. The other drains it back out.
Both are tools central banks use, and they work in opposite directions.
Quantitative easing means a central bank buys bonds with newly created money.
That extra money flows into banks, pushing interest rates down.
Lower rates make borrowing cheaper and often push stock prices up.
Quantitative tightening means the central bank lets those bonds mature or sells them.
Money leaves the system, and interest rates tend to rise again.
Borrowing gets more expensive, and asset prices often cool down.
The difference is direction: one grows the money supply, the other shrinks it.
You feel this in mortgage rates, savings interest, and how expensive loans become.
Same tool, opposite direction, one purpose: controlling how much money moves.
