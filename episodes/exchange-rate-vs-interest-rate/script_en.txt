One tells you what your money is worth abroad. The other, what it earns at home.
Both move every day, and both are shaped by forces most people never see.
The exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, like dollars per euro.
If the dollar rises against the euro, American goods sold in Europe get pricier.
The interest rate is the price of borrowing money, largely set by central banks.
A higher rate makes saving more rewarding and borrowing more costly.
The difference: one prices money against other money, the other prices money over time.
Central banks often raise interest rates to attract foreign money and lift the exchange rate.
Together they explain why your paycheck buys more some years than others abroad.
