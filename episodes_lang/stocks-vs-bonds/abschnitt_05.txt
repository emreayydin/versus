Stocks and bonds both create tax bills and trading costs, but the type and timing of the tax differs.
Buying or selling shares can trigger a broker fee, though many platforms now offer commission free trading.
Selling stocks for a profit usually creates a capital gains tax, based on how long they were held.
Bond interest, often called a coupon, is usually taxed as ordinary income rather than a capital gain.
Government bonds sometimes carry tax advantages, depending on the country and the type of bond issued.
Corporate bonds usually don't get these breaks, so their interest gets taxed like regular income.
Dividends from stocks can also be taxed, sometimes at a different rate than capital gains.
Funds holding either asset may charge ongoing management fees, quietly reducing returns over time.
Both instruments can sit inside tax sheltered accounts, softening the bite for patient investors.
Selling bonds before maturity can also create a taxable gain or loss, much like selling stocks.
Careful record keeping matters for both, since accurate cost basis tracking makes tax time simpler.
Tax rules vary widely by country, so the exact numbers always depend on local law.
